# Fair market value of gala benefits

An organization may use any reasonable method to estimate the fair market value (FMV) of goods or services it provided to a donor, as long as it applies the method in good faith. The IRS examples: a lesson a pro sells for $100 is worth $100.00, a museum room is valued at a comparable ballroom's $2,500.00, and a tour never sold commercially is worth $0, so a $1,000.00 payment for it is deductible in full.

## The three IRS examples, worked

Values from the IRS quid pro quo page; deductible parts worked out by the checker.

| Benefit | Paid | Good faith estimate | Deductible |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Tennis lesson with a pro | $500.00 | $100.00 | $400.00 |
| Private event in a museum room | $50,000.00 | $2,500.00 | $47,500.00 |
| Evening tour with a well-known artist | $1,000.00 | $0.00 | $1,000.00 |

- A charity provides a one-hour tennis lesson with a tennis professional for the first $500 payment it receives. The tennis professional provides one-hour lessons on a commercial basis for $100. A good faith estimate of the lesson's FMV is $100.
- If the hotel ballroom rents for $2,500, a good faith estimate of the FMV of the right to hold the event in the museum is $2,500.
- A good faith estimate of the FMV of the evening museum tour is $0 even though the artist conducts it.

## For a gala

A gala ticket's value is what the guest gets: dinner and drinks at what a comparable public venue would charge, the entertainment at a comparable ticket price, any gift at its retail price. If there is an established charge for the event, that charge is the value of your benefit. If there is no established charge, the reasonable value of the right to attend the event is the value of your benefit.

## Steps

1. **Find the established charge.** If the event or item has a regular public price, that price is its value (IRS Publication 526).
2. **Otherwise price something comparable.** The organization may estimate the FMV of goods or services that generally are not commercially available by using the FMV of similar or comparable goods or services.
3. **Add up what each guest receives.** Dinner, drinks, entertainment, valet, gifts: everything that comes with the ticket, valued at what a buyer would pay, not what it cost you.
4. **Write it down and use it consistently.** Keep the method and the comparable prices with the event file; the receipt prints the total as your good faith estimate.

## Questions

### Is fair market value what the charity paid for the dinner?

Not necessarily. It is what a buyer would pay for the same thing in an ordinary sale; the IRS examples price comparable commercial services.

### Can the value be zero?

A good faith estimate of the FMV of the evening museum tour is $0 even though the artist conducts it.
