IRS rules

What a giving statement must include

A year-end giving statement that serves as the IRS written acknowledgment must show the organization's name, the amount of each money contribution, a description (not the value) of any property given, and a sentence saying no goods or services were provided, or describing them with a good faith estimate of their value, or saying only intangible religious benefits were provided (IRS Publication 1771); the donor's Social Security number is not needed.

The goods-or-services sentence

Every statement carries one of these, worked out from the gifts on it:

Read the rest: The goods-or-services sentence
  • Nothing given in return: "No goods or services were provided in exchange for these contributions."
  • A church giving only intangible religious benefits: "The only goods or services provided in exchange for these contributions were intangible religious benefits." If a religious charitable organization provides only "intangible religious benefits" to a donor, the contemporaneous written acknowledgment does not need to describe or value those benefits. It can simply state that the organization provided intangible religious benefits to the donor.
  • Something given in return (a dinner, a ticket): "Riverside Food Bank provided goods or services in exchange for the contribution marked *, described with our good faith estimate of their fair market value on that line. No goods or services were provided in exchange for the other contributions." "The amount of a contribution that is deductible for federal income tax purposes is limited to the excess of the money (and the value of any property) you gave over the value of the goods or services we provided."

What counts as intangible religious benefits: Examples include admission to a religious ceremony and a de minimis tangible benefit, such as wine used in a religious ceremony. Benefits that are not intangible religious benefits include education leading to a recognized degree, travel services, and consumer goods.

The $250 rule

A donor cannot claim a federal income tax deduction for any single contribution valued at $250 or more unless the donor obtains a contemporaneous written acknowledgment of the contribution from the donee charitable organization. An organization that does not acknowledge a contribution incurs no penalty; but, without a contemporaneous written acknowledgment, the donor cannot claim the federal income tax deduction.

So a church whose donor gave $250 or more in one gift must make sure that donor has a statement in time; the statement the tool makes says so in a line of its own. In the church template, the Smith family's $300.00 building-fund gift is the one that needs it: 54 gifts totalling $3,520.00, of which 1 was $250 or more.

A donor cannot claim a federal income tax deduction for any monetary contribution unless the donor maintains a record of the contribution or a timely written communication from the charitable organization showing the name of the organization, the date of the contribution, and the amount of the contribution.

Questions

Can one year-end statement count as the IRS acknowledgment?

Yes. Pub 1771: "One contemporaneous written acknowledgment, such as an annual summary, may be used to substantiate several single contributions valued at $250 or more."

Does a giving statement need the donor's Social Security number?

No. Pub 1771: "It isn't necessary to include either the donor's Social Security number or tax identification number on the contemporaneous written acknowledgment."

Is there an IRS form for the statement?

No. "There are no IRS forms for the contemporaneous written acknowledgment." Letters, postcards, or computer-generated forms with the above information are acceptable.

Should a church put a value on donated items?

No. Pub 1771 asks for "a description (but not the fair market value) of any contribution of property".

Do weekly offerings under $250 need a statement?

Not for the $250 rule: "Separate contributions of less than $250 will not be aggregated. An example of this could be weekly offerings to a donor's church of less than $250 even though the donor's annual total contributions are $250 or more." Donors still need a bank record or a written communication for every money gift, so most churches send everyone a statement.

Can giving statements be emailed?

Yes: An organization can provide the acknowledgment electronically, such as via an email addressed to the donor.
