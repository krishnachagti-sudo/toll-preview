# Late payment interest for freelancers and sole traders

Yes, when the client is a business: a sole trader is a business under the law. A £2,400 invoice received September 1, 2026 with no agreed terms was due in 30 days, and on November 8 it carries £29.36 statutory interest at 11.75% plus £70.

- £2,400, Nov 8: £29.36

## Why sole traders count

GOV.UK applies the right when "another business is late paying for goods or a service". The directive behind the 2013 rules defines an undertaking as any organisation acting in its economic or professional activity, "even where that activity is carried out by a single person".

## When it does not apply

- The client is a private person (a consumer).
- Your contract sets its own late-payment rate.
- The invoice is disputed and paid late because of the dispute.

## Checklist before you write

- The invoice date the client received it, and the agreed terms (or 30 days).
- The invoice total with VAT.
- Proof of delivery or completion.
- Any emails agreeing a later date.
- The interest and fixed sum, worked out to the day you send the letter.

> Agency, umbrella-company and overseas-client arrangements change who the debtor is and which law applies; they are not covered here.

## Please read

- Not legal advice. The figures follow the official rules and rates quoted, read on the date shown; a court decides any dispute.
- Business-to-business debts only: the UK Act, the Irish regulations, § 288(2) BGB and the EU directive do not cover money owed by consumers.
- Statutory interest does not apply where your contract sets its own late-payment rate (UK: a "substantial remedy"); use your own terms instead.
- No interest or compensation is worked out for an invoice paid late because of a genuine dispute.
- US public-body rules (federal, Texas, Florida local government) apply only to payments by those bodies; between businesses in the US, only the rate in your agreed terms applies, within your state's limits.
