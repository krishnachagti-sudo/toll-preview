What the totals follow

Each workweek stands alone

Federal overtime is at least time and a half for hours over 40 in a workweek. A workweek is a fixed, recurring 168 hours that may start on any day and at any hour, and hours may not be averaged over two or more weeks (29 CFR 778.104 and 778.105). A biweekly sheet of 30 and 50 hours has 10 hours of overtime, not none. A shift that runs past the start of the workweek is split at that moment.

Rounding has to even out

29 CFR 785.48(b) accepts recording start and stop times to the nearest 5 minutes, tenth or quarter hour only if, over time, it does not fail to pay for all the time actually worked. The check above rounds the punches you type and shows, punch by punch, which way each moved and what it did to the total. At the quarter hour, rounding to the nearest is often called the 7-minute rule: 1 to 7 minutes past rounds down, 8 to 14 rounds up.

Breaks and the clock

A bona fide meal period is not work time and is ordinarily 30 minutes or more (29 CFR 785.19); rest periods of 5 to about 20 minutes must be counted as hours worked (785.18), so a short unpaid gap is flagged. A shift past midnight counts into the next morning. With a time zone, the night clocks change is counted in real time: 10 PM to 6 AM is 9 hours when clocks fall back, 7 when they spring forward.

  • Totals are worked out from the times entered. Overtime follows the federal rule of over 40 hours in a fixed workweek (FLSA) unless you set other thresholds; a state law, contract or policy may require more.
  • The rounding check shows what rounding does to these punches. It does not decide whether a rounding practice is lawful.
  • Gross pay uses the hourly rate entered; the regular rate for overtime can include other pay, such as some bonuses, that is not counted here. Not legal or payroll advice.
  • Sources, read November 7, 2026: 29 CFR 785.48, 785.18, 785.19, 778.104, 778.105, DOL Fact Sheet #23 (revised October 2019) and Fact Sheet #21 (revised July 2008).
