Where the number comes from

The default is the rate in the European Central Bank's own published table for that day, set at about 16:00 Central European Time on every TARGET working day. A day with no fixing uses the latest fixing in the 7 days before it, and the answer names that day.

What it does not cover

  • The rate a bank or card will exchange at: these are reference rates, the record of the day.
  • HMRC's weekly amendments, which are not in its monthly files.
  • A working day before its 16:00 fixing: it gets no rate rather than an older one.
Read how the rate is worked out

One fixing a day

The ECB sets reference rates for about 30 currencies against the euro at about 16:00 Central European Time on every TARGET working day. The default answer is the rate in the ECB's own published table for that day.

Pairs without the euro

For two other currencies the page divides one euro rate by the other, on the same day's fixing, then rounds to 6 decimal places. The rounding is exact, with halves rounded away from zero, and the inverse is worked out from the unrounded rate.

Days with no fixing

Weekends and TARGET holidays have no fixing. The tool uses the latest fixing up to 7 days before the date you ask for. If there is none in that window, or the date is before 4 January 1999, it says so and you are not charged.

A working day the ECB has not published yet, such as today before the 16:00 fixing, gets no rate rather than an older one. Ask again after the fixing. That run is free too.

Source: European Central Bank euro foreign exchange reference rates. The ECB publishes them "for information purposes only": they are the record of the day's rate, not a price a bank will exchange at. If your tax office names its own rates, choose that source above. Currencies the ECB no longer publishes, such as the Croatian kuna, can still be asked for through the API on dates when it did.

Which rate to use: ECB, US Treasury, IRS or HMRC

Which rate to use

Use the rate the authority you report to asks for. Each answer names the publication and the period it used. What each authority says about its own rates:

ECB reference (default)

Daily rates against the euro since 1999. The ECB says they are "published for information purposes only."

US Treasury reporting rate

Quarterly rates in units per US dollar, which U.S. government agencies use to convert amounts "as of the date of this report and for the ensuing three months." When current rates differ from a published rate by 10% or more, Treasury issues an amendment with a new effective date, and the tool uses it from that date. For the FBAR, FinCEN points to the rate for 31 December. Pairs must include USD.

IRS yearly average

One rate per currency per year, in units per US dollar, for the year of your date. The IRS says it "has no official exchange rate" and "accepts any posted exchange rate that is used consistently." In general it says to use the spot rate when you receive, pay or accrue an item. Publication 54 says to report amounts in U.S. dollars and points to these pages. Pairs must include USD.

HMRC monthly rate

Monthly rates in units per pound, from January 2021. HMRC says they "cover the period from the first to the last day of the next calendar month" and that you can use them "for duty and import VAT purposes." When the commercial rate differs by more than 5% from a published rate, HMRC updates that rate the following week; those weekly amendments are not in the monthly files this tool reads. Pairs must include GBP.
