Why two sources give two exchange rates for the same day

Each source fixes its rate at a different time, for a different purpose, and rounds differently. Converted at four official rates, $10,000 on 31 December 2025 is between £7,426.38 and £7,618.47, a spread of £192.09.

SourceWhich figurePounds per dollar$10,000 in pounds
ECB reference rateFixing of 31 December 2025 (euro rates, crossed)0.742638£7,426.38
U.S. Treasury reporting rateReport of 31 December 2025, published as 0.743 pounds per dollar0.743£7,430.00
IRS yearly averageAverage for 2025, published as 0.759 pounds per dollar0.759£7,590.00
HMRC monthly rateDecember 2025, published 19 November 2025 as 1.3126 dollars per pound0.761847£7,618.47

Every figure is from the tool's engine and the agencies' published files. The HMRC rate is published as dollars per pound, so its amount is the dollars divided by it.

Why do they differ?

  • Time of fixing: the ECB sets one rate each working day in the early afternoon in Frankfurt; Treasury's is a quarterly reporting rate; HMRC sets a month's rate in the month before.
  • Period: the IRS figure is a yearly average, so it reflects the whole year, not the last day.
  • Direction and rounding: Treasury and the IRS publish units per dollar to three or four places; HMRC publishes dollars per pound; the ECB publishes units per euro, so a dollar to pound rate is a cross of two figures.
  • Purpose: each is meant for its own reports and filings. None is a rate you can trade at.

Which one should I cite?

The one your rule or contract names, with its date and source. For US filings see which rate to use for US taxes; for UK VAT see which rate a foreign-currency invoice uses. If nothing names a source, pick one, say which, and use it every time.

Questions

Which exchange rate is the correct one?

The one your rule names. The IRS accepts any posted rate used consistently, the FBAR uses the Treasury rate for 31 December, and UK VAT uses the UK market selling rate or HMRC's period rate. Cite the source and date you used.

Why is my bank's rate different again?

A bank quotes the rate it buys or sells at, usually with a margin, at the moment it converts. The figures on this page are reference or reporting rates, not trading rates.
