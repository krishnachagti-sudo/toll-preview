A homeowner's guide to preliminary notices

A preliminary notice is not a lien: it tells you that a subcontractor or supplier is working on your property and could claim a lien if your contractor does not pay them. The statutory forms tell you how to protect yourself: Arizona's points to conditional and unconditional waivers, Washington's to joint checks and lien releases, and Florida's warns that without a release each time you pay you may pay twice (A.R.S. 33-992.01(D); RCW 60.04.031(4); Fla. Stat. 713.06(2)(c)).

What the forms tell owners

  • Arizona: "this is not a lien"; require a conditional waiver before you pay and an unconditional waiver after (A.R.S. 33-992.01(D)). Arizona lien waivers.
  • Washington: "THIS IS NOT A LIEN"; pay with joint checks or require lien releases from everyone who sent you a notice (RCW 60.04.031(4)).
  • Florida: "TO AVOID A LIEN AND PAYING TWICE, YOU MUST OBTAIN A WRITTEN RELEASE FROM US EVERY TIME YOU PAY YOUR CONTRACTOR." (Fla. Stat. 713.06(2)(c)). Florida lien waivers.

What not to do

  • Do not ignore it: keep it with your contract and check it against the waivers you collect.
  • Do not pay the sender directly unless your contract and state law allow it; Florida requires 10 days' written notice to the contractor first (713.06(3)(g)).
  • Do not make the final payment without the waivers from everyone who sent a notice.

Where an owner-occupant has extra protection

  • Arizona: on an owner-occupant's dwelling, only someone with a written contract directly with the owner can claim a lien (A.R.S. 33-1002(B)).
  • Washington: on an existing owner-occupied home, liens of those without a contract with you are paid only from what you had not yet paid the prime when the notice arrived (RCW 60.04.031(3)(b)).
  • Maryland: on a single family dwelling built for your own residence, a subcontractor's lien cannot exceed what you still owe the contractor (9-104(f)(3)).

Not legal advice and not a law firm. Dates are worked out from each state's own statute text, quoted with the date we read it. Deadlines are strict: a lien recorded one day late is usually lost. Act before the date shown, and earlier when it falls on a weekend or holiday. For disputes, large amounts or anything unusual about your job, ask a construction attorney licensed in the project's state.
